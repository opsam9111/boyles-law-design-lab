Read the full transcript
  1. A gas contains particles moving in every direction.
  2. When the same gas is pushed into a smaller volume, the particles have less space.
  3. They collide with the container walls more frequently, so pressure increases.
  4. At constant temperature, pressure and volume have an inverse relationship. This is Boyle's law.

The learning challenge

How can students understand something they cannot see?

01

The misconception

Students may memorize P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ without understanding why pressure rises when a gas is compressed.

02

The learning goal

Manipulate volume, observe pressure, identify the inverse relationship, and explain the pattern using particle collisions.

03

The context

Beginning physics students learn face to face through prediction, a needle-free syringe demonstration, digital exploration, and explanation.

The three design lenses

Each framework asks a different question.

Together, the frameworks help the designer move from choosing technology to designing a coherent and accessible learning experience.

T

Does everything fit?

TPACK

TPACK focuses on the interaction of content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and technological knowledge within a particular teaching context.

  • Content: Boyle's inverse pressure–volume relationship.
  • Pedagogy: Predict, investigate, discuss, graph, and explain.
  • Technology: A dynamic model that exposes invisible particle behavior and generates data.

Design decision: Use the simulation because its affordances support inquiry into this specific concept—not simply because it is digital.

(Koehler & Mishra, 2009; Koehler et al., 2014)

S

What changes through technology?

SAMR

SAMR describes technology use as substitution, augmentation, modification, or redefinition. It can prompt reflection on how a task changes.

  1. S Digital questions replace a paper worksheet.
  2. A Immediate feedback and audio add functional improvement.
  3. M Students manipulate volume, collect live data, and revise explanations.
  4. R Students publish accessible multimedia explanations for an authentic audience.

Design decision: Use the level that serves the learning goal. “Higher” technology use is not automatically better.

(Hamilton et al., 2016)

U

Who might be excluded?

UDL

UDL anticipates learner variability and reduces barriers through multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression.

  • Engagement: Prediction, choice, relevance, collaboration, and manageable challenge.
  • Representation: Physical model, animation, numbers, formula, text, audio, and graph.
  • Action and expression: Written, visual, oral, audio, or video explanation.

Design decision: Combine the simulation with descriptions, transcripts, sentence frames, tactile experience, and response choices.

(CAST, 2024; Michela, n.d.)

Same scenario, different guidance

Compare the decisions—not just the definitions.

How TPACK, SAMR, and UDL guide decisions in the Boyle's law lesson
Design decision TPACK lens SAMR lens UDL lens
Why use a simulation? Its dynamic particle model represents the science content and supports guided inquiry. It modifies the task by allowing repeated manipulation, observation, and data collection. It adds a visual and numerical representation, but alternative descriptions are still needed.
How should students participate? Pair investigation supports the selected collaborative pedagogy. Shared data and revision meaningfully change the original individual worksheet task. Roles, choices, sentence frames, and individual think time reduce participation barriers.
How should learning be assessed? The assessment must reveal conceptual and mathematical understanding of Boyle's law. Technology can support rapid feedback and multimedia production, but novelty is not the goal. Students may explain through equivalent written, visual, oral, audio, or video formats.
What if technology fails? Return to the syringe, particle cards, and teacher-guided data because pedagogy and content remain central. A non-digital task can still achieve the goal; a lower SAMR level is not automatically inferior. Offer printed, tactile, verbal, and partner-supported routes to the same learning goal.

Comparative insight

TPACK evaluates coherence among what is taught, how it is taught, and the tool used. SAMR makes the degree of task change visible. UDL asks whether every learner can access, participate in, and express learning within that redesigned task. The frameworks are complementary, not interchangeable.

Accessible lesson blueprint

From curiosity to evidence-based explanation

  1. Engage

    Predict with a syringe

    Students predict what happens to trapped air as a sealed, needle-free syringe is compressed.

    Choice: speak, write, or draw the prediction.
  2. Explore

    Manipulate the model

    Pairs change volume, observe pressure, and record at least four trials while other variables remain constant.

    Roles: operator, recorder, narrator, and evidence checker.
  3. Explain

    Connect particles to pattern

    Students graph the results and explain why collision frequency changes as available space changes.

    Support: vocabulary bank, worked example, and sentence frames.
  4. Apply

    Transfer to real life

    Students apply the relationship to a bicycle pump, breathing, tire inflation, or another selected context.

    Choice supports interest and transfer.
  5. Demonstrate

    Show understanding

    Students submit an equivalent written, diagrammed, oral, audio, or captioned-video explanation.

    One goal; multiple valid forms of expression.

Common success criteria

  • Accurately identifies the inverse pressure–volume relationship.
  • Uses evidence from recorded values or the graph.
  • Explains the pattern through particle-wall collisions.
  • Applies the relationship to a new situation.

Evidence and access

Designed transparently

Accessibility statement

This resource uses semantic headings, keyboard-operable controls, high-contrast text, visible focus indicators, live text updates, reduced-motion support, responsive layouts, and optional speech. The same core relationship is communicated through movement, numbers, words, and a formula.

The external PhET experience may present additional accessibility considerations. Teachers should test it with the technologies used by their learners and retain the tactile, verbal, numerical, and printed alternatives in the lesson.

Design limitation

The on-page model is intentionally simplified. It illustrates an inverse mathematical relationship rather than reproducing a full molecular simulation. It should support—not replace—teacher explanation, physical investigation, and scientific discussion.
